Something I find interesting about Planck’s radiation law is the order in which the pieces appeared. The successful formula came before the energy-element argument that made it such a consequential result. It is often described as a fortunate guess. But what kind of guess was it?

A modern blackbody derivation starts with quantised mode energies and calculates the spectrum. That is a useful modern explanation. It should not be mistaken for a transcript of how Planck reached the formula in 1900.

## Two limits, written in modern notation

Here, <span class="math" markdown="0">\(u_\nu\)</span> is energy per unit volume per unit frequency, and <span class="math" markdown="0">\(x=\frac{h\nu}{k_{\mathrm B}T}\)</span>. Planck’s spectrum is:

<div class="equation" tabindex="0">
\[
u_\nu=\frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3(e^x-1)}.
\]
</div>

For small <span class="math" markdown="0">\(x\)</span>, the exponential has the expansion <span class="math" markdown="0">\(\exp(x)=1+x+\frac{x^2}{2}+\cdots\)</span>. Keeping the leading term in the denominator gives:

<div class="equation" tabindex="0">
\[
x\ll1:\qquad u_\nu\approx\frac{8\pi\nu^2k_{\mathrm B}T}{c^3}.
\]
</div>

This is the Rayleigh–Jeans limit. At large <span class="math" markdown="0">\(x\)</span>, subtracting 1 hardly changes <span class="math" markdown="0">\(\exp(x)\)</span>, giving the Wien form:

<div class="equation" tabindex="0">
\[
x\gg1:\qquad u_\nu\approx\frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}e^{-x}.
\]
</div>

“Low frequency” is therefore shorthand for low relative to a temperature-dependent scale. The same frequency can fall into different regimes as the temperature changes. The approximation is controlled by a dimensionless ratio, not by a universal boundary between red and blue light.

## What was being guessed in October 1900?

Planck had been trying to justify Wien’s distribution through thermodynamics and a model of resonators interacting with radiation. Measurements at long wavelengths, including those of Rubens and Kurlbaum, showed that Wien’s expression could not describe the whole spectrum. His [paper presented on **19 October 1900**](https://uni-tuebingen.de/fileadmin/Uni_Tuebingen/Fakultaeten/MathePhysik/Institute/IAP/Forschung/MOettel/Geburt_QM/planck_VerhDPG_2_202_1900.pdf) proposed an improved formula. The paper itself explains the change through the entropy of a resonator, rather than through the familiar modern sum over photon occupations.

It is tempting to retell this as a direct interpolation between two finished textbook formulas. That compresses a more complicated history. In particular, the modern ultraviolet-catastrophe story is a clear way to expose the failure of classical equipartition; it is not the problem statement that should simply be placed in Planck’s mouth in October 1900.

## A short reconstruction of the entropy idea

Here is a way to see the mathematical structure in modern symbols. Let <span class="math" markdown="0">\(U\)</span> be a resonator’s mean energy and <span class="math" markdown="0">\(S\)</span> its entropy. Thermodynamics gives <span class="math" markdown="0">\(\frac{\mathrm dS}{\mathrm dU}=\frac{1}{T}\)</span>. Consider the following curvature, with a positive energy scale <span class="math" markdown="0">\(\varepsilon\)</span>:

<div class="equation" tabindex="0">
\[
\frac{\mathrm d^2S}{\mathrm dU^2}=-\frac{k_{\mathrm B}}{U(U+\varepsilon)}.
\]
</div>

At small <span class="math" markdown="0">\(U\)</span>, its dependence is proportional to <span class="math" markdown="0">\(-\frac{1}{U}\)</span>; at large <span class="math" markdown="0">\(U\)</span>, it behaves as <span class="math" markdown="0">\(-\frac{1}{U^2}\)</span>. Those different entropy curvatures connect the two limiting behaviours within one expression.

Integrating once, with <span class="math" markdown="0">\(\frac{\mathrm dS}{\mathrm dU}\)</span> tending to zero as <span class="math" markdown="0">\(U\)</span> grows, gives <span class="math" markdown="0">\(\frac{1}{T}=\frac{k_{\mathrm B}}{\varepsilon}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{U}\right)\)</span>. Solving for the mean energy then yields:

<div class="equation" tabindex="0">
\[
U=\frac{\varepsilon}{\exp\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{k_{\mathrm B}T}\right)-1}.
\]
</div>

With <span class="math" markdown="0">\(\varepsilon=h\nu\)</span>, this is precisely the mean thermal energy used in the modern derivation. The calculation explains why a simple entropy interpolation can lead to the right functional form. It does **not** establish that this is the only possible interpolation, or supply the microscopic reason for choosing a finite energy scale.

## The formula still needed an explanation

The next step was the statistical counting argument presented on **14 December 1900**, developed in the [1901 paper](https://uni-tuebingen.de/fileadmin/Uni_Tuebingen/Fakultaeten/MathePhysik/Institute/IAP/Forschung/MOettel/Geburt_QM/planck_AnnPhys_309_553_1901.pdf). Planck counted distributions of finite energy elements among resonators and related the count to entropy. The size of an element had to remain proportional to frequency, <span class="math" markdown="0">\(\varepsilon=h\nu\)</span>.

That statement should not be silently expanded into the whole later theory of photons. As [MIT’s historical discussion notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/sts-042-einstein-oppenheimer-feynman-physics-in-the-20th-century-fall-2020/mitsts_042j_f20_lecnote_blackbody.pdf), how Planck understood the physical status of his counting procedure is a historical question; the modern mode-occupation calculation and Planck’s original resonator argument are related without being identical.

## A modern connection: Padé approximation

There is a useful mathematical connection here. If only a few terms of a function’s expansion are known, a Taylor polynomial is one way to use them. A **Padé approximant** uses a ratio of polynomials instead. A denominator can represent a pole, and its degree can change the large-argument behaviour—possibilities that a Taylor polynomial alone does not have.

For a series <span class="math" markdown="0">\(f(z)=c_0+c_1z+\cdots\)</span>, the <span class="math" markdown="0">\([m/n]\)</span> approximant, when it exists with this normalisation, is:

<div class="equation" tabindex="0">
\[
\begin{aligned}
R_{m,n}(z)&=\frac{P_m(z)}{Q_n(z)},\qquad Q_n(0)=1, \\
Q_n(z)f(z)-P_m(z)&=O\!\left(z^{m+n+1}\right).
\end{aligned}
\]
</div>

The numerator and denominator have degrees at most <span class="math" markdown="0">\(m\)</span> and <span class="math" markdown="0">\(n\)</span>. Their coefficients are chosen so that the rational function reproduces the known series through order <span class="math" markdown="0">\(m+n\)</span>. This is coefficient matching, rather than a least-squares fit to measured points; [NIST’s definition](https://dlmf.nist.gov/3.11#iv) makes the matching condition precise. Information at another expansion point or at infinity can also be built into a rational interpolation, but ordinary one-point Padé matching does not automatically impose it.

### Try it on the thermal factor

The factor <span class="math" markdown="0">\(F(x)=\frac{x}{e^x-1}\)</span> is the mean thermal energy of a mode divided by <span class="math" markdown="0">\(k_{\mathrm B}T\)</span>. Its apparent singularity at zero is removable. Expanding around that point gives:

<div class="equation" tabindex="0">
\[
F(x)=1-\frac{x}{2}+\frac{x^2}{12}-\frac{x^4}{720}+O(x^6).
\]
</div>

For a <span class="math" markdown="0">\([1/1]\)</span> approximant, write <span class="math" markdown="0">\(\frac{1+ax}{1+bx}\)</span>. Expanding this ratio and matching the coefficients of <span class="math" markdown="0">\(x\)</span> and <span class="math" markdown="0">\(x^2\)</span> gives <span class="math" markdown="0">\(a-b=-\frac12\)</span> and <span class="math" markdown="0">\(b^2-ab=\frac1{12}\)</span>. Thus <span class="math" markdown="0">\(b=\frac16\)</span> and <span class="math" markdown="0">\(a=-\frac13\)</span>:

<div class="equation" tabindex="0">
\[
[1/1]_F(x)=\frac{1-\tfrac{x}{3}}{1+\tfrac{x}{6}}.
\]
</div>

This reproduces the three coefficients used to construct it. Yet it becomes negative for <span class="math" markdown="0">\(x>3\)</span> and tends to <span class="math" markdown="0">\(-2\)</span> at large <span class="math" markdown="0">\(x\)</span>, while the exact thermal factor stays positive and decays as <span class="math" markdown="0">\(xe^{-x}\)</span>. That is a useful failed extrapolation: matching a local series does not guarantee the right behaviour everywhere. A fixed finite rational function cannot reproduce that exponential tail asymptotically. Extra knowledge about the physical limits matters.

### How close is this to Planck’s interpolation?

The rational structure appears in the **entropy curvature**, not in the exponential spectrum itself. Set <span class="math" markdown="0">\(y=\frac{U}{\varepsilon}\)</span> and remove the dimensions from the earlier curvature:

<div class="equation" tabindex="0">
\[
\begin{aligned}
C(y)&=-\frac{\varepsilon^2}{k_{\mathrm B}}\frac{\mathrm d^2S}{\mathrm dU^2} \\
&=\frac{1}{y(1+y)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
</div>

After factoring out the small-energy <span class="math" markdown="0">\(\frac1y\)</span> divergence, the remaining function is <span class="math" markdown="0">\(yC(y)=\frac1{1+y}\)</span>: a <span class="math" markdown="0">\([0/1]\)</span> rational form. It connects the two powers needed for the entropy curvature. Using information from both ends is closer in spirit to **two-point rational interpolation** than to constructing a Padé approximant from a single Taylor series.

This is a retrospective mathematical comparison: Planck’s argument interpolated entropy behaviour, while the standard Padé construction matches series coefficients. The two limiting powers constrain an interpolation without selecting a unique function. The useful connection is the modelling strategy: choose a quantity whose limits can be joined by a simple expression, then seek a physical explanation for why it works.

## Where this enters theoretical physics

### Quantum mechanics and perturbation theory

An energy level may be known only as a perturbation series in a coupling <span class="math" markdown="0">\(g\)</span>. Directly adding terms can become unhelpful when the series diverges or the coupling is no longer very small. Padé approximants reorganise the available coefficients into a rational function that can sometimes reach further. A classic concrete example is the **anharmonic oscillator**: [Loeffel, Martin, Simon and Wightman (1969)](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/esnpf-h2238) established convergence results for Padé approximants to its energy levels. Such results depend on the particular problem’s analytic structure.

### Critical phenomena and Dlog-Padé

Near a continuous phase transition, suppose a susceptibility behaves as <span class="math" markdown="0">\(\chi(g)\approx A\left(1-\frac{g}{g_c}\right)^{-\gamma}\)</span>. A rational function cannot exactly represent a general non-integer power singularity. Its **logarithmic derivative**, however, has a simple leading pole:

<div class="equation" tabindex="0">
\[
\frac{\mathrm d\ln\chi}{\mathrm dg}\approx\frac{\gamma}{g_c-g}.
\]
</div>

Dlog-Padé approximates this derivative from its series. A pole can estimate the critical location <span class="math" markdown="0">\(g_c\)</span>, and its strength can estimate the exponent <span class="math" markdown="0">\(\gamma\)</span>; in the usual <span class="math" markdown="0">\(\frac1{g-g_c}\)</span> convention, the residue is **<span class="math" markdown="0">\(-\gamma\)</span>**. [Pelizzola’s Ising-model study](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.53.5825) is a concrete application to critical behaviour. This is another example of choosing a better quantity to approximate before building the rational function.

### Quantum field theory and Padé–Borel summation

For perturbative coefficients that grow roughly factorially, one can first form the **Borel transform** <span class="math" markdown="0">\(B(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_nt^n}{n!}\)</span>, then use Padé to continue that transformed series. Where the continuation and integral are well defined, the inverse step is:

<div class="equation" tabindex="0">
\[
f_{\mathrm B}(g)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-t}B(gt)\,\mathrm dt.
\]
</div>

Padé–Borel replaces <span class="math" markdown="0">\(B\)</span> in this integral with a rational approximant. [Ellis and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-ph/9509312) applied Padé approximants and Borel transforms to the **Bjorken sum rule in QCD**. The transform does not make every field-theory series unambiguous: singularities on the integration path require a summation prescription. [Pindor’s discussion](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/9903151) addresses precisely this complication.

Across these applications, nearby approximant orders, unwanted poles and known physical limits all provide useful checks. A pole of an approximant is not automatically a physical resonance or phase transition. The point is to extract more from limited information while continuing to test the result against the physics.

What I like about the story is the role of a well-chosen constraint. Whether we are interpolating an entropy curvature or constructing a Padé approximant, the useful questions are similar: which information have we used, which limits survive, and what would show the approximation has failed? In Planck’s case, finding an expression that survived the checks opened another question: why did that expression describe nature?
